# Sweep deconvolution and the impulse response — decisions

*2026-08-30. Lane L4a, station 2. Written from the station-1 research pass. The
sweep generator and its Farina inverse filter already exist and are tested
(`core/include/rta/gen/Sweep.h`); nothing in `core/` yet turns a captured
response into an impulse response. This record exists so the implementation
cannot silently choose otherwise.*

L4 as written in `docs/plans/MASTER-EXECUTION-PLAN.md` gathers ten features. It
is split here the way L5 had to be split:

| | scope | layer | blocked by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L4a** | deconvolution → IR, frequency response from the IR, polarity (G21) | `core/` | — |
| **L4b** | ETC, Schroeder, Lundeby, EDT/T20/T30, C50/C80/D50 | `core/` | L4a |
| **L4c** | draggable IR gate (G25), min/excess phase (G24), offline WAV (G22) | `app/` | L4a, L4b |
| **L4d** | STI/STIPA (G4) | — | **purchase of IEC 60268-16** |

This record covers **L4a only**. L4b and L4c get their own; L4d is not scheduled.

Ten decisions follow. Each records what the rejected option would have cost.

**Every number here is reproducible by one of three committed commands**, none
of which writes anything:

```
.venv/Scripts/python.exe tools/probe_sweep_fade.py          # pulse geometry, fade law
.venv/Scripts/python.exe tools/verify_l4a_pulse.py          # sections A B C F I J
.venv/Scripts/python.exe tools/verify_l4a_measurement.py    # sections D E G H K
```

The last two exist because the first draft of this record asserted several
things no measurement had been run for. **Four did not survive being measured,
and are marked in place rather than quietly corrected:**

| | what the draft said | what the measurement said |
|---|---|---|
| dec. 4 | a unity path reads 0 dB flat in band | **−7.06 … +2.40 dB** at the shipped fade widths, and the quoted band was computed at the wrong duration |
| dec. 6 | the confidence figure is how the caller finds out | it is the **highest** reading on the one case that answers wrongly |
| dec. 7 | transform from `originIndex` | that slices the analysis pulse in half — **+23.5 dB** error on a highpass |
| dec. 8 | float32 and float64 agree | the comparison had been run in a way that could not have disagreed |

Two more claims were guesses that the measurements then split down the middle:
decision 5's explanation of the fade asymmetry was **half confirmed and half
refuted**, and decision 8's refusal to locate the precision crossover turned out
to be over-cautious — there isn't one in reach.

A record that hides its own corrections teaches the next reader that this kind of
work does not produce any. Decision 5's central claim was also put deliberately
at risk — an experiment designed to kill it — and survived; that is recorded in
place too, because a decision nobody tried to falsify is a decision nobody
tested.

## 1. Linear deconvolution by convolution with an inverse filter

**Decision.** The impulse response is `y ⊛ inv`, a **linear** (non-cyclic)
convolution computed by FFT with zero padding to `nextPow2(Ny + Ninv − 1)`.
There is no spectral division anywhere in L4a.

**Why not spectral division.** Müller & Massarani's own recommendation is
`IFFT(Y/X)` with both signals zero-padded to double length. It is the more
general method and it is what most Python implementations do — `spatialaudio/sweep`'s
`deconv_process()` is exactly `irfft(rfft(y, N) / rfft(x, N))` with
`N = pow2(len(x) + len(y) − 1)`. It is rejected for one specific reason: **X is
near zero outside `[f1, f2]`, and every implementation therefore needs a
regularisation term.** That term is a number somebody picks. No standard
defines it, no closed form produces it, and no test in this repo could prove a
chosen value correct. This project's verification standard forbids exactly that
kind of constant. Convolution with the Farina inverse filter needs no such term
because it never divides by anything.

**What that costs, stated plainly.** Müller criticises the Farina construction
directly: deconvolving with only the time-inverted, amplitude-shaped stimulus
leaves errors near the start and end frequencies of the sweep, because the
sweep's switch-on and switch-off ripple the extremities of its spectrum. He is
right, and decision 5 turns his qualitative warning into a measured number and
a fix. His own remedy — invert a *measured* reference spectrum obtained by
looping the output back to the input — is better, and is not available: the
physical loopback cable is task **M7** in `docs/reports/T12-hardware-run.md`,
still waiting on a human. Decision 2 is what keeps that door open.

## 2. The inverse filter is an argument, never a construction

**Decision.** `Deconvolver` takes `std::span<const float> inverseFilter`. It
does not own a `Sweep`, does not include `rta/gen/Sweep.h`, and has no idea how
the filter was made.

**Why this is the most important line in the design.** Today the caller passes
`Sweep::buildInverseFilter()`. The moment M7's loopback cable exists, Müller's
reference-measurement method becomes available, and adopting it must be a change
of *argument*, not a change of code. A `Deconvolver` that constructed its own
filter internally would have to be rewritten to accept the better method — and
a class that must be rewritten to accept a known improvement is a class that
will still be there in a year, holding the improvement out.

The rejected alternative — `Deconvolver(const Sweep&)` — reads more convenient
at exactly one call site and forecloses the method the literature prefers.

## 3. Nothing is chopped, and the origin is stated rather than assumed

**Decision.** The result carries the **whole** linear convolution, plus the
index at which `t = 0` sits, plus `L`:

```
struct Deconvolution {
    std::vector<float> samples;      // length Ny + Ninv - 1, nothing removed
    std::size_t        originIndex;  // = Ninv - 1
    double             sampleRate;
    double             normalisationGain;  // the scalar already applied
    double             harmonicSpacingL;   // L = T / ln(f2/f1)
};
```

**Where `originIndex = Ninv − 1` comes from.** `inv[m] = s[Ns−1−m]·e[m]`. At
convolution lag `k = Ns−1`, every term of `Σ_j s[j]·inv[k−j]` becomes
`s[j]²·e[Ns−1−j]` — all non-negative, the one lag at which the sum adds
coherently. So the analysis pulse peaks at `Ns−1`, and since `Ninv = Ns`, that
is `Ninv − 1`. Measured, not assumed, by `tools/probe_sweep_fade.py`: peak index
95999 for a 2 s sweep at 48 kHz (`N−1 = 95999`), sign **positive**, and
symmetric about the peak to 3.0e−5 of peak amplitude over ±2 ms.

**Why nothing is chopped.** Müller's advice is to discard the negative-time
half. That is correct for room acoustics and wrong for this repo, because the
same region *is* the distortion measurement. The Nth harmonic packet sits at

    Δt_N = −L · ln(N),        L = T / ln(f2/f1)

which follows in closed form: the Nth harmonic's instantaneous frequency at time
`t` equals the fundamental's at `t + L·ln N`, so deconvolution — which maps the
fundamental at `t` onto zero — maps the harmonic onto `−L·ln N`. Müller's own
worked example agrees (a sweep at 100 Hz/100 ms and 200 Hz/200 ms puts H2 at
−100 ms, and `L·ln 2 = 100 ms`). Verified numerically against a square-law and
a cubic nonlinearity: **H2 lands at 0.0 samples of error, H3 at −0.1**. Two
orders rather than one, so the *logarithmic* spacing is exercised and not
merely a single offset that any monotone law would have fitted.

Discarding that region would make G3 (THD readout) and P4b re-measure what was
already captured. `harmonicSpacingL` is carried so a later lane needs no access
to the `Sweep` that produced the data.

## 4. Normalisation: one in-band scalar, with the band derived from the fades

**Decision.** `normalisationGain` is chosen so the mean of
`|FFT(sweep ⊛ inv)|` over the valid band equals 1. A unity path then reads 0 dB
in band **to within the flatness that band actually has** — which is a measured
quantity, not the zero this decision originally claimed.

**Why the band is not a chosen constant.** The fades are the reason the band
edges are unreliable, so the band is defined by where the fades have finished:

    f_lo = f1 · exp(fadeInSec / L)
    f_hi = f2 · exp(−fadeOutSec / L)

Every figure is read off the `Sweep::Config` the caller already supplied.

**The correction this decision needed.** A first draft claimed a unity path
reads "0 dB flat in band" and quoted a band of 28.3 Hz … 18665 Hz. Both were
wrong. The band figure mixed the fade seconds of one configuration with the
duration of another — `Sweep::Config`'s shipped defaults give 21.4 Hz … 19725.6 Hz
— and the flatness was never measured at all. Measured
(`tools/verify_l4a_pulse.py` section C):

| configuration | valid band | deviation across it |
|---|---|---|
| shipped defaults, T = 10 s (0.10/0.02 oct) | 21.4 – 19725.6 Hz | **−7.06 … +2.40 dB** |
| T = 2 s, fades 0.50/0.50 oct | 28.3 – 14142.1 Hz | −1.40 … +1.25 dB |
| T = 2 s, fades 2.00/0.50 oct | 80.0 – 14142.1 Hz | **−0.01 … +0.03 dB** |

So the band formula defines where the answer is *meaningful*; it does not by
itself make the answer *flat*. Flatness is bought by decision 5's fade widths,
and at the widths this repo ships today the "0 dB" reading is worth ±7 dB at the
bottom of the band. Any claim about absolute level must quote the figure for the
configuration in use, and the implementation should expose the flatness it
achieved rather than let a caller assume it is zero.

**The rejected alternative and its exact defect.** Normalising by the *peak* of
`sweep ⊛ inv` is one line shorter and wrong at the third decimal: the peak
height depends on band-edge phase as well as in-band gain, so "0 dB" would be
approximately rather than derivably true, and no test could say by how much.

**What must not be attempted.** Dividing by the *spectrum* of `sweep ⊛ inv`
rather than by a scalar would flatten the response outside `[f1, f2]` too — and
would reintroduce division by near-zero, i.e. decision 1's regularisation
parameter through the back door. A scalar cannot do that.

## 5. The fade-in clamp is in the wrong units, and that costs 33 dB

**Decision.** `Sweep::Config` gains `fadeInOctaves` (default **2.0**), and the
clamp becomes

    fadeInSamples = round(fs · max(fadeInSec, 2/startHz, fadeInOctaves·ln2·L))

The existing `2/startHz` floor stays — it guards against a click at switch-on,
which is a different failure — but it stops being the only floor.

**What the research found.** The pre-arrival floor of `sweep ⊛ inv` (the
sidelobe level more than 50 ms before the peak, relative to the peak) is a
function of fade width measured in **octaves of sweep travel**, and is
independent of sweep duration in seconds. Measured with `tools/probe_sweep_fade.py`,
20 Hz–20 kHz at 48 kHz, at both T = 2 s and T = 10 s, agreeing within 1 dB:

| fade-in (octaves) | floor | | fade-out (octaves) | floor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | −74.0 dB | | 0.25 | −109.6 dB |
| 0.50 | −75.4 dB | | 0.50 | −108.7 dB |
| 1.00 | −84.5 dB | | 1.00 | −106.9 dB |
| 1.50 | −98.0 dB | | 2.00 | −103.3 dB |
| 2.00 | **−108.7 dB** | | 3.00 | −99.9 dB |
| 3.00 | −122.9 dB | | | |

(T = 2 s column; the T = 10 s column agrees within 1 dB and is in the script's
output.) The two fades pull in **opposite directions**: a wider fade-in improves
the floor by roughly 19 dB per octave, a wider fade-out degrades it by roughly
3.5 dB per octave.

**Why they differ — half the hypothesis confirmed, half refuted.** This record
first guessed that the inverse filter's +6 dB/oct envelope was the whole story:
it sits at maximum exactly where the reversed fade-*out* lands and at minimum
where the fade-*in* lands, a weighting ratio of `f2/f1` = **60 dB** for a
20 Hz–20 kHz sweep. That is a testable prediction — strip the envelope and the
two fades should behave alike. Tested by rebuilding the inverse filter as plain
time reversal (`verify_l4a_pulse.py` section J):

| widened fade | 0.50 → 2.00 oct, envelope on | same, plain reversal |
|---|---|---|
| fade-in | −75.4 → −108.7 — **helps 33.3 dB** | −36.4 → −69.6 — **helps 33.2 dB** |
| fade-out | −75.4 → −70.0 — **hurts 5.4 dB** | −36.4 → −35.5 — neutral, 0.9 dB |

- **Confirmed:** the envelope is what makes a wide fade-out actively *harmful*.
  Without it, widening the fade-out is merely useless rather than damaging.
- **Refuted:** it does not explain the fade-in at all. Widening the fade-in buys
  the same 33 dB with the envelope and without it, so that benefit comes from
  softening the low-frequency spectral edge itself — a mechanism the envelope
  plays no part in.

**Two mechanisms, one of them now identified.** Stated as what was tested, which
is less than the original guess claimed and more than it had earned.

**Why the current clamp is a defect and not merely a default.** `2/startHz` is
a rule in *cycles at f1*, i.e. in seconds. The quantity that governs the floor
is octaves. For a 2 s sweep the clamp yields 0.5 octave; for a 10 s sweep the
same 0.1 s is 0.07 octave. So under today's code **lengthening the sweep makes
the band edges worse** — the opposite of what every operator will expect, and
invisible to the current test suite because no test looks at negative time. The
resulting −75 dB is below the >90 dB Müller states is needed for a
high-quality room impulse response.

**It is not numerical error.** Computed in float64 and float32 the floor is the
same to 0.1 dB. See decision 8.

**The obvious objection, tested and refuted.** A wide fade-in costs
low-frequency energy, so it is only worth paying if the artefact is *inside* the
band of interest. If the sidelobes lived below 20 Hz, band-limiting would remove
them for nothing and this decision would be over-engineering. That is the check
that would have killed it. Measured per octave band — filter-free, comparing the
pre-arrival segment against an equal-length segment centred on t = 0
(`verify_l4a_pulse.py` section A), as pre-arrival energy relative to the same
band of the main pulse:

| band | fade-in 0.5 oct | fade-in 2.0 oct |
|---|---|---|
| 10 – 20 Hz | −58.5 dB | −44.0 dB |
| **20 – 40 Hz** | **−71.5 dB** | **−88.9 dB** |
| 40 – 80 Hz | −78.5 dB | −105.8 dB |
| 80 – 160 Hz | −91.7 dB | −119.3 dB |
| 320 – 1280 Hz | −123.8 dB | −150.1 dB |
| 5120 – 20480 Hz | −189.1 dB | −200.3 dB |

The artefact is worst at **20–40 Hz — inside the band of interest, not below
it.** Band-limiting cannot remove it. The objection fails and the decision
stands. (Below 20 Hz the wide fade reads *worse* because it deliberately
suppresses the sweep's own energy there; that band is outside the measurement
and the ratio is not meaningful in it.)

**And the practice that looked like a substitute is not one.** A secondary guide
to AES-2id recommends starting the sweep below the band of interest — 10 Hz
rather than 20 Hz. Compared at constant sweep rate so only one thing changes
(section B), as in-band pre-arrival energy over 20 Hz – 20 kHz:

| | floor |
|---|---|
| f1 = 20 Hz, fade 0.5 oct — today's default | −99.9 dB |
| f1 = 10 Hz, fade 0.5 oct — the AES-2id practice | −91.0 dB |
| f1 = 5 Hz, fade 0.5 oct | −93.9 dB |
| f1 = 20 Hz, fade 2.0 oct | **−132.0 dB** |
| f1 = 5 Hz, fade 2.0 oct | −120.8 dB |

Lowering `startHz` alone made the artefact **worse**, not better. There is no
contradiction with the guide: its stated reason for starting low is "to ensure
the useful band has full energy" — a **signal-to-noise** measure, addressing a
different problem than pre-arrival artefacts. Two knobs, two problems. Reading
one as a substitute for the other is the mistake this measurement prevents.

**The trade-off is the caller's to make, so it is exposed.** A wide fade-in
tapers the bottom of the sweep: at 2 octaves from 20 Hz, everything from 20 to
80 Hz is amplitude-shaped, costing signal-to-noise exactly where room modes
live. Three legitimate positions exist and `fadeInOctaves` plus `startHz`
select between them:

- **Both, at the cost of a longer sweep** — `startHz` two octaves below the band
  of interest (5 Hz) *and* `fadeInOctaves = 2`. The fade then covers 5–20 Hz, so
  the whole band of interest is at full amplitude and the floor is −120.8 dB.
  This is the configuration to recommend to an operator measuring a room.
- **Dynamic range first** (the default, `fadeInOctaves = 2` from 20 Hz):
  −132.0 dB, with 20–80 Hz tapered.
- **Low-frequency energy first** (`fadeInOctaves = 0.5`): −99.9 dB in band, full
  amplitude from 28 Hz. Correct for a quick check.

**Consequence for existing tests.** `test_generator_sweep.cpp` asserts
`fadeInSamples()` equals the `2/startHz` clamp. Whether that assertion still
holds depends on which of the three floors dominates for its fixture; the
implementation plan must re-derive it rather than adjust it until green.

## 6. Polarity: peak-relative first arrival, with a refusable confidence

**Decision.** `Polarity` returns a sign, the index it decided on, and a
confidence. It reports `Unknown` rather than guessing when confidence is low.

The first arrival is the first index at or after `originIndex` whose magnitude
reaches `arrivalFraction` (default 0.5, i.e. −6 dB) of the largest magnitude in
the search window. The sign is that sample's sign.

**Why the threshold is a Config field and not a constant.** It is a convention,
not a derivation — the patents surveyed variously use "first peak above a
threshold" and "sign of the maximum after band-pass and exponential weighting".
A convention hard-coded is a convention nobody can check; exposed, a test can
drive it.

**Why the noise estimate has a derivable window.** Confidence is peak magnitude
over the RMS of the region between `originIndex − L·ln 2` and `originIndex`.
That window is free of harmonic packets by decision 3's formula — H2 is the
first packet and it sits at exactly `−L·ln 2` — so it contains noise and
sidelobes only. The window is computed, not chosen.

**Why the answer is meaningful at all.** The analysis pulse was measured
symmetric about its peak to 3.0e−5 and peaks **positive** (decision 3), so it is
zero-phase to within that error, and the sign of the arrival is the sign of the
system. A pulse that was not zero-phase would make polarity a matter of opinion.

**The failure it was tested against.** The rule "first sample reaching a
fraction of the peak" can in principle latch onto a *precursor* sidelobe of the
opposite sign, reporting the polarity backwards — the worse failure, because a
confident wrong answer sends someone to rewire a working loudspeaker. Driven
through three systems of increasing hostility at four thresholds each, both
polarities (`verify_l4a_measurement.py` section E):

| system | first arrival, fractions 0.2 → 0.7 | sign |
|---|---|---|
| full range, 60 Hz – 15 kHz | +0 … +1 samples | correct at all four |
| horn, 500 Hz – 8 kHz | +1 … +2 samples | correct at all four |
| subwoofer, 30 – 120 Hz | +67 … +158 samples | correct at all four |

The subwoofer is the case that should break it — a 90 Hz-wide passband rings for
tens of milliseconds and has large excursions of both signs — and the sign
survives. Note how far the *index* moves with the threshold there (67 to 158
samples, 1.4 to 3.3 ms): the reported arrival time from a narrowband system is
threshold-dependent and must not be quoted as a delay. The **sign** is what this
class promises.

**The non-minimum-phase case, which this record first left untested.** Five more
systems, both polarities each (`verify_l4a_measurement.py` section H). "Symmetry" is
the largest positive excursion over the largest negative one in the arrival
window — near 0 dB means the waveform has no dominant sign at all:

| system | sign read | confidence | symmetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| minimum phase `[1, 0.5]` | correct | 66.9 dB | 9.0 dB |
| maximum phase `[0.5, 1]` | correct | 71.1 dB | 10.3 dB |
| 2nd-order allpass | correct | 61.5 dB | 2.1 dB |
| inverted reflection, 1.5× the direct, 3 ms later | correct | 67.7 dB | 3.5 dB |
| **narrowband, 200 – 250 Hz** | **WRONG** | **129.7 dB** | **0.1 dB** |

Two things follow, and the second is a defect in this decision as first written.

1. Non-minimum phase alone does **not** break it. Maximum phase, a true allpass,
   and an inverted reflection louder than the direct sound all read correctly —
   the 50% threshold latches onto the direct arrival before the reflection lands.
2. **The confidence figure does not detect the one failure. It is the highest
   reading in the table.** Confidence as decision 6 defines it measures
   signal-to-noise, and a narrowband system has a superb signal-to-noise ratio
   and no definable polarity. Answering "is this loud enough to trust?" is not
   answering "does this waveform have a sign?"

**So the class reports the symmetry figure too, and returns `Unknown` on it.**
Default threshold 1.0 dB, exposed like `arrivalFraction` and for the same
reason. **The margin is thin and the record must not pretend otherwise**: the
failing case reads 0.1 dB and the nearest passing case, the allpass, reads
2.1 dB. Five systems is not a survey. Treat 1.0 dB as a starting value with
evidence behind it, not as a validated boundary, and widen the survey before
anyone relies on it.

**What is still untested.** A device whose passband excludes the sweep's
mid-band entirely; a multi-way loudspeaker measured off-axis, where the drivers'
arrivals separate. Untested, not proven safe.

## 7. Frequency response: start **before** t = 0, by two cycles of `f_lo`

**Decision.** `IrSpectrum` transforms from `originIndex − round(2·fs/f_lo)` to
the end of the buffer, and returns complex bins. Phase stays in **radians** in
`core/`.

**Why the window does not start at t = 0, which is what this decision first
said.** The analysis pulse is symmetric about its peak (decision 3 measures the
symmetry at 3.0e−5). So the deconvolved signal `h ⊛ p` has real content on
*both* sides of `originIndex`, and a transform beginning exactly there slices
the pulse down the middle. That is not a subtle error. Recovering two known
filters and comparing against their analytic responses over 100 Hz – 10 kHz
(`verify_l4a_measurement.py` section D):

| lead-in kept | 2nd-order 1 kHz lowpass | 4th-order 300 Hz highpass |
|---|---|---|
| **0 ms (the naive window)** | +1.33 … +2.55 dB | **−0.09 … +23.48 dB** |
| 5 ms | −1.23 … +1.05 dB | −0.02 … 0.00 dB |
| 20 ms | −0.02 … +0.02 dB | 0.00 dB |
| 50 ms | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB |

A 23 dB error on a highpass, from a windowing choice, on a path whose pure-delay
round trip was exact to the sample and to four decimal places of gain. The delay
test passed because it only inspected the peak — **a test that looks only at the
peak cannot see a window that damages the skirts.**

**Why two cycles rather than 20 ms.** Milliseconds would be a constant somebody
picked. The pulse's width is set by the bottom of the valid band, so the lead-in
is expressed in cycles of `f_lo` and read off the same Config as everything
else. Measured against the 300 Hz highpass at two fade widths, worst-case error
across the band:

| `f_lo` | 1 cycle | 2 cycles | 4 cycles | 8 cycles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 28.3 Hz | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB |
| 80.0 Hz | 0.01 dB | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB | 0.00 dB |

Two cycles is one doubling past where the error vanishes at both widths. It also
echoes the `2/startHz` fade floor already in `Sweep` — the same "two cycles of
the lowest frequency that matters" reasoning, which is not a coincidence: both
are asking how long the slowest thing in the signal takes.

**What the caller must be told.** The returned spectrum's time reference is the
start of that window, not `t = 0`, so it carries a linear phase term of
`2·fs/f_lo` samples. Either `IrSpectrum` removes it or it reports it. Leaving it
implicit is how a group-delay readout acquires a constant offset nobody can
find.

**Why no user-adjustable window.** A window over the IR is a gate, and the gate
is L4c's draggable one (G25). Shipping a fixed *gate* here would mean L4c must
undo a decision this lane had no reason to make. The lead-in above is not a gate
— it is the minimum window that does not corrupt the answer, and there is no
setting of it that a user would want smaller.

**Why radians.** `app/` converts to degrees exactly once, in `Analyser::pushPair`
(see `docs/dsp/2026-08-29-display-layer-l5c.md`). A second conversion point in
`core/` is how a factor of 57.3 gets applied twice.

## 8. float32 is not the limiting error, and this is why we checked

**Decision.** The existing `RealFft` (single precision) is used. No
double-precision FFT is written for the offline path.

**Why, with the number.** The pre-arrival floor computed **natively** in float32
and in float64 agrees to 0.0 dB at 0.5, 2.0 and 3.0 octaves of fade-in —
including at −122.9 dB. So single precision is not the limit anywhere in the
range decision 5 makes reachable, and writing a double-precision transform would
improve a term that is not binding. The repo's existing note that `RealFft` is
single precision (`memory/float32-fft-precision.md`) is why this was checked
rather than assumed.

**How this claim was wrong the first time.** The original measurement computed
the whole convolution in double precision and then cast the *result* to float32.
That measures double precision and rounds the answer; it says nothing about
single-precision arithmetic, and the agreement it "found" was guaranteed by
construction. `numpy.fft` silently promotes float32 input to float64, which is
what made the mistake invisible. The corrected measurement uses `scipy.fft`,
which honours the input dtype, and `tools/l4a_sweep.py` asserts the
intermediate spectrum is `complex64` before believing the comparison — because a
precision test that has silently stopped testing precision still prints a
reassuring number.

**Where it becomes the limit: nowhere reachable.** An earlier version of this
decision refused to guess and left the crossover unmeasured. It has now been
looked for along both axes that could move it — fade width, which lowers the
sidelobe floor, and transform size, which raises the arithmetic noise
(`verify_l4a_pulse.py` section I):

| fade-in | float64 | float32 | | sweep length | transform | difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 oct | −108.7 | −108.7 | | 2 s | 2¹⁸ | 0.0 dB |
| 4 oct | −133.9 | −133.9 | | 8 s | 2²⁰ | 0.0 dB |
| 6 oct | −147.7 | −147.7 | | 32 s | 2²² | 0.0 dB |

Agreement to 0.1 dB down to **−147.7 dB** and up to a **2²² transform** — beyond
anything this lane can produce, since a 6-octave fade-in on a ten-octave sweep
tapers most of the signal away. Single precision is not the limit anywhere in
the reachable space, and the "must re-measure past 3 octaves" caution the
earlier draft carried can be retired.

Worth noting for decision 5's benefit: the floor **saturates near −134 dB at
4 octaves** and buys little after that, at any sweep length. The fade law is not
a straight line forever.

**One consequence for the implementation.** The FFT is sized
`nextPow2(Ny + Ninv − 1)`, which for a 10 s sweep and a 12 s capture at 48 kHz
is 2²¹ = 2 097 152. `RealFft` builds tables of N/2 complex entries, so that one
transform costs about 8 MB of tables plus the working buffers. Acceptable
offline, and a reason this path must never be constructed on the audio thread.

## 9. The capture must outlast the sweep by 1.5 × the reverberation time

**Decision.** `Deconvolver` takes the captured buffer as given, but the record
states the rule the caller must satisfy, and the class exposes enough for the
caller to check it: the recording must continue past the end of the sweep by at
least **1.5 × the expected RT60**.

**Why a measured rule rather than a quoted one.** The rule "post-measurement
quiet time must exceed the expected reverberation time" comes from a secondary
guide to AES-2id, not from the standard and not from anything measured here.
Measured against a synthetic room with an RT60 of exactly 1.0 s — band-limited
exponentially decaying noise plus a clear direct arrival — recovering its
frequency response over 100 Hz – 10 kHz (`verify_l4a_measurement.py` section K):

| gap after the sweep | gap / RT60 | rms error | worst error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 s | 0.00 | 1.46 dB | **32.55 dB** |
| 0.25 s | 0.25 | 0.34 dB | 21.90 dB |
| 0.50 s | 0.50 | 0.07 dB | 4.61 dB |
| 1.00 s | 1.00 | 0.00 dB | 0.60 dB |
| **1.50 s** | **1.50** | 0.00 dB | **0.02 dB** |
| 2.00 s | 2.00 | 0.00 dB | 0.01 dB |

The guide's rule is right and this sharpens it. Note how badly **rms error
misleads** here: at half the RT60 the rms is already 0.07 dB — looks finished —
while the worst bin is still out by 4.61 dB. An average taken over a spectrum
hides exactly the narrow-band errors a truncated decay produces. One doubling
past `gap = RT60` is where the worst case falls under 0.1 dB, and that is the
number to publish.

**What the operator sees if they ignore it.** Truncating the decay is a
rectangular window on the tail, so the error is a rippling comb, worst at low
frequencies where the decay is longest — indistinguishable, on screen, from
room modes. It will be read as a room problem and someone will try to EQ it.

## 10. How CI proves this with no sound card

Eight assertions, none of which reads back a value the implementation printed:

1. **Closed form.** The analysis pulse peaks at `Ninv − 1` with a positive sign.
2. **Closed form.** `Δt_N = −L·ln N` for N = 2 and 3 against a synthetic
   memoryless nonlinearity, tolerance ±1 sample.
3. **Round trip, two shapes and two windows.** A pure delay must come back at
   the exact index with the exact gain. A 4th-order 300 Hz highpass must match
   its analytic magnitude to better than 0.1 dB over 100 Hz – 10 kHz **with**
   decision 7's lead-in, and must be visibly wrong without it. The second half
   of that is the test that would have caught the naive window; a round-trip
   test that only checks the peak passes a broken one.
4. **Derived band.** A unity path reads flat across `[f_lo, f_hi]` to the
   deviation decision 4 tabulates for the configuration under test — ±0.03 dB at
   2.00/0.50 octaves. Asserting a tighter figure than the configuration can
   deliver would be asserting the fade widths, not the normalisation.
5. **Regression lock, labelled as one.** The pre-arrival floor at
   `fadeInOctaves = 2` is at or below −100 dB, and at 0.5 is worse than −80 dB.
   These bracket the measured law in decision 5; they are locks against an
   empirical table, not a closed form, and `tools/probe_sweep_fade.py` is
   committed so the table can be re-derived rather than trusted.
6. **Polarity, and the case it must refuse.** `+s` yields `+1` and `−s` yields
   `−1` for a full-range, a horn, a subwoofer, a maximum-phase pair, an allpass
   and an inverted reflection louder than the direct sound. A **200–250 Hz
   narrowband** system must yield `Unknown` — not a sign. That last one is the
   assertion with teeth: it is the case that reads a *confident wrong answer*
   under the confidence metric alone, so a suite without it would pass an
   implementation that misleads an operator into rewiring a working box.
7. **Capture length.** A synthetic room with a known RT60, deconvolved from a
   capture truncated at `gap = 0.5 × RT60`, must show a worst-bin error above
   1 dB, and at `1.5 × RT60` must show one below 0.1 dB. Asserting only the
   second would pass an implementation that never noticed the first.
8. **Golden vector.** `tools/gen_golden.py` gains a case generated with
   numpy/scipy in the checkout's `.venv`.

Plus one count that must be *read*, not assumed: `core_has_no_framework_deps`
scans 70 files today, and must scan 70 + n after this lane. Trap #1 in
`docs/HANDOFF.md` is that a mis-globbed guard passes while watching nothing.

## What this record does not decide

- **ETC, Schroeder integration, Lundeby truncation, RT60, clarity.** L4b.
  One thing L4a must hand it, measured here so it is not rediscovered as a
  bug: Müller warns that linear deconvolution turns steady noise into a
  decaying, progressively low-pass filtered tail that reads as reverberation.
  With white noise at about −34 dB added to the captured sweep
  (`verify_l4a_measurement.py` section G), 100 ms slices of the deconvolved result:

  | after t = 0 | level | spectral centroid |
  |---|---|---|
  | 100 ms | −78.1 dB | 7722 Hz |
  | 400 ms | −87.0 dB | 3239 Hz |
  | 800 ms | −99.2 dB | 1322 Hz |
  | 1400 ms | −117.0 dB | 1605 Hz |

  **A decay of 30.0 dB/s whose centroid falls with it, produced by a
  measurement of nothing at all.** Read as a room it reports RT60 = 2.00 s,
  shortening with frequency — an entirely plausible answer with no room in it.
  (The centroid rising again at 1400 ms is the level reaching the arithmetic
  floor, not a feature.)

  **And the rate is not accidental.** The inverse filter's +6 dB/oct envelope
  spans exactly `20·log10(f2/f1)` dB across the sweep's length, and the
  deconvolved noise inherits that envelope, so

      apparent RT60 ≈ 3·T / log10(f2/f1)

  For the case above — three decades in 2 s — that is `RT60 = T` exactly, which
  is the sort of coincidence that gets promoted to a law. Tested against two
  further configurations to stop that happening: T = 4 s over three decades
  predicts 4.00 s and measures 3.58 s (−10.5%); T = 2 s over two decades
  predicts 3.00 s and measures 3.21 s (+6.8%). **The mechanism is derived, the
  magnitude is good to about 10% over that range, and the residual is
  unexplained.** L4b should use it to *recognise* the artefact, never to
  subtract it — and must find its own truncation point rather than trust the
  visible decay.
- **The draggable gate, min/excess phase, offline WAV.** L4c.
- **STI/STIPA.** Blocked on IEC 60268-16.
- **THD from the harmonic packets.** G3/P4b. L4a only guarantees the packets
  survive and that `harmonicSpacingL` locates them.
- **Whether the app offers a loopback reference measurement** (Müller's
  preferred method). That needs M7's cable to exist first, and decision 2 is
  what makes adopting it cheap when it does.
